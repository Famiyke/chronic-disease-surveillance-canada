# Prevention Investment Brief
## Canadian Chronic Disease Surveillance — Key Findings for Decision-Makers
**Prepared for:** Provincial Public Health Director
**Data source:** PHAC Canadian Chronic Disease Surveillance System (CCDSS), 2010–2022
**Analysis date:** 2024

---

## What This Brief Covers
Prevalence trends for five chronic conditions across all 10 Canadian provinces and four age groups, drawn from PHAC CCDSS surveillance data. This brief identifies where the burden is highest, which conditions are growing fastest, and where prevention investment will have the greatest impact.

---

## The Three Things That Matter Most

### 1. Mental health conditions are now a chronic disease crisis

Mood and anxiety disorders grew 51% between 2010 and 2022 — faster than diabetes, hypertension, COPD, or heart failure. One in nine Canadians (11.3%) now lives with a diagnosed mood or anxiety disorder.

This matters beyond the mental health system. People with mood and anxiety disorders are significantly more likely to have comorbid physical chronic conditions, use emergency services more frequently, and have worse outcomes from conditions like diabetes and heart disease. Treating this as a separate system from physical chronic disease is costing the system substantially.

**What to do:** Fund integrated care clinics where mental health and chronic disease management are delivered together, not in separate silos.

---

### 2. Atlantic Canada is not catching up — it is falling further behind

All four Atlantic provinces (NL, NB, NS, PE) exceed the national average across every condition tracked. NL has the highest burden nationally. The Atlantic average sits 1.5 percentage points above the national average, and that gap has been consistent across every year in the dataset.

| Province | Avg Prevalence 2022 | vs National |
|----------|--------------------|-|
| NL | 17.4% | +2.4pp |
| NB | 16.4% | +1.4pp |
| NS | 16.3% | +1.4pp |
| PE | 15.7% | +0.7pp |
| National | 15.0% | — |

The Atlantic gap is not explained by age demographics alone. It reflects structural gaps in primary care access, prevention infrastructure, and social determinants of health.

**What to do:** Atlantic provinces need targeted investment in community health workers, primary care capacity, and structured prevention programs — particularly for diabetes and hypertension, where the gap is widest.

---

### 3. The seniors care system is not ready for what is coming

Heart Failure prevalence in the 75+ age group is 96 times higher than in the 20–44 group. COPD is 30 times higher. Hypertension is 14 times higher. These are not gradual transitions — they are cliff edges in demand.

Canada's demographic shift means the 75+ population will grow substantially over the next 15 years. Acute care hospitals are already under pressure. Community-based cardiac and respiratory support infrastructure that does not yet exist in most provinces needs to be built before that wave arrives. This infrastructure takes 5–7 years to build and staff.

**What to do:** Begin planning and funding community cardiac and respiratory support programs now, with a 10-year horizon. Provinces that wait until the demand arrives will face a decade of acute care overflow.

## What a 1% Reduction in Diabetes Prevalence Would Mean

Diabetes is the second-fastest growing condition nationally and the most expensive in terms of downstream costs — hospital admissions, kidney disease, cardiovascular events, and prescription drug utilisation.

A 1 percentage point reduction in national diabetes prevalence through structured lifestyle and nutrition programs would prevent approximately 380,000 cases. At an estimated average cost of $5,500 per person per year in direct and indirect health costs (CIHI), this represents approximately **$2.1 billion in annual savings**.

Published evidence from comparable Canadian lifestyle intervention programs shows a 1–2pp reduction is achievable within 5 years in targeted populations.

## What This Analysis Cannot Tell Us

- Whether prevalence increases reflect better diagnosis rates or true disease growth
- Whether provincial differences are driven by demographics, environment, or access gaps
- Individual-level risk factors — this is population-level surveillance data

Patient-level analysis requires access to CCDSS microdata through a formal PHAC data sharing agreement.
